Governance & Accountability:
Who Is Responsible for Cybersecurity?

Governance defines direction and decision authority. Accountability connects cybersecurity responsibilities to oversight, evidence, and consequences.

Read the articleAll Insights

Friday Framework 002 · Governance & Accountability · Michael Costner · November 13, 2026

Organizations establish cybersecurity policies, deploy security controls, assign administrative responsibilities, and invest in technologies intended to protect their operations. But when a cybersecurity decision must be made, who has the authority to make it, who accepts the associated risk, and who is accountable for the outcome?

These questions introduce one of the foundational disciplines of cybersecurity frameworks: governance and accountability.

What is cybersecurity governance?

Cybersecurity governance establishes how an organization directs, oversees, and evaluates its cybersecurity activities. It connects security objectives to business priorities, defines decision-making authority, establishes risk expectations, and provides oversight of how cybersecurity responsibilities are fulfilled.

Governance is not simply the existence of policies or the assignment of a security officer. It is the structure through which an organization determines what must be protected, who has authority to make decisions, what constitutes acceptable risk, and how leadership evaluates whether expectations are being met.

Without effective governance, cybersecurity can become a collection of independently managed technical activities rather than a coordinated organizational responsibility.

Responsibility, authority, and accountability

  • Responsibility: Who is expected to perform an activity or fulfill an obligation?
  • Authority: Who is permitted to make decisions, approve actions, or accept risk within defined boundaries?
  • Accountability: Who must answer for decisions, oversight, and outcomes?

Consider an organization implementing multifactor authentication. An IT administrator may be responsible for configuring and maintaining the technology. A security manager may evaluate exceptions. An authorized business or risk owner may have authority to accept documented residual risk. Executive leadership retains governance responsibilities for ensuring that appropriate authority, oversight, and risk-management structures exist.

Performing a security task does not automatically grant authority to accept its business consequences. Likewise, delegating implementation does not necessarily eliminate leadership's accountability for oversight.

How established frameworks address governance

NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0

NIST CSF 2.0 explicitly recognizes governance through its GOVERN function, addressing organizational context, risk management strategy, roles, responsibilities, policy, oversight, and cybersecurity supply chain risk management.

ISO/IEC 27001:2022

ISO/IEC 27001:2022 establishes information security management system requirements that include leadership commitment, information security policy, assigned responsibilities, risk management, performance evaluation, and continual improvement.

COBIT 2019

COBIT 2019 provides a governance and management framework for enterprise information and technology, emphasizing enterprise objectives, stakeholder needs, decision structures, and the distinction between governance and management activities.

CIS Controls v8.1

CIS Controls v8.1 primarily organizes prioritized cybersecurity safeguards, including practices related to policies, assigned responsibilities, and security management processes. It does not serve the same enterprise governance purpose as COBIT or the ISMS requirements of ISO/IEC 27001.

These approaches are not identical or necessarily competing. Each contributes a different perspective on how organizations can establish, implement, and evaluate cybersecurity responsibilities.

When governance exists on paper but not in practice

Imagine an organization discovers that a critical business application depends on an unsupported operating system. The technical team identifies the exposure and recommends remediation. The business unit explains that replacing the system would disrupt operations. The organization decides to continue operating the application.

  • Who authorized that decision, and did that individual have the authority to accept the risk?
  • Was the associated risk evaluated, and were alternative safeguards considered?
  • Was the decision assigned an expiration or review date?
  • Could the organization demonstrate the answers afterward?
An undocumented assumption of authority is not the same as an accountable risk decision.

This is why governance requires more than policies, meetings, and organizational charts. The existence of a process does not automatically establish that its decisions were appropriate or its intended outcomes achieved.

The JAZB perspective

The JAZB Cybersecurity Framework Core v1.2.0 approaches governance and accountability as part of its broader enterprise cybersecurity assurance methodology. Its published research emphasizes defined obligations, accountable decision-making, and evidence-based assessment.

Identifying a responsible individual is only part of the governance question. An assessment must also consider whether the relevant obligation was established, whether decision-making authority was appropriate, and whether available evidence supports the conclusion being reported.

For example, a completed risk acceptance form may demonstrate that a decision was documented. It does not necessarily establish that the approving individual possessed the required authority, understood the material consequences, or fulfilled applicable review obligations.

These principles are not exclusive to JAZB. Established frameworks already address governance, authority, risk ownership, and oversight. JAZB's research explores a particular integration of these considerations into assessable enterprise cybersecurity obligations, without asserting universal superiority.

The central lesson

Cybersecurity governance determines how an organization directs its security responsibilities. Accountability determines who must answer for the decisions and outcomes associated with those responsibilities. Together, they help ensure that cybersecurity decisions are not made solely because someone has the technical ability to execute them.

A mature cybersecurity program must explain not only what was done, but why it was authorized, who was accountable, and what evidence supports the resulting assurance claims.

If a significant cybersecurity incident occurred tomorrow, could your organization demonstrate who authorized the decisions that contributed to its exposure, what information supported those decisions, and who was accountable for reviewing them?

That is where governance moves beyond documentation and becomes an operational responsibility.
