Governance in Practice:
Policies, Authority, and Oversight

How organizations translate governance principles into defined expectations, legitimate decisions, delegated authority, and meaningful oversight.

Read the articleAll Insights

Friday Framework 003 · Governance in Practice · Michael Costner · November 20, 2026

In last week's Friday Framework, we explored governance and accountability and their importance within cybersecurity frameworks. This week, we examine how those principles translate into organizational practice.

Organizations often associate governance with policies, committees, regulatory obligations, and executive oversight. While each contributes to governance, their existence alone does not establish that an organization is effectively governed.

Effective governance requires more than defining expectations. It requires establishing who has the authority to make decisions, how that authority is exercised, and how the organization ensures that actions remain consistent with its established objectives and boundaries.

Policies Establish Organizational Expectations

Policies are among the most recognizable components of organizational governance. They establish expectations for how an organization operates, protects its assets, manages risk, and fulfills its obligations.

Within cybersecurity, policies may address areas such as:

  • Information security and data protection.
  • Identity and access management.
  • Acceptable use of organizational resources.
  • Risk management and acceptance.
  • Change management.
  • Incident response and escalation.
  • Third-party and supplier relationships.
  • Artificial intelligence use and oversight.

However, establishing a policy is only the beginning.

An organization may have a policy prohibiting unauthorized access to sensitive information, but unless appropriate processes, technical controls, and accountability mechanisms support that policy, its effectiveness remains uncertain.

Policies establish what should happen. Governance establishes how those expectations are directed, maintained, and overseen.

This distinction is important when evaluating cybersecurity frameworks. The presence of a documented policy may satisfy a documentation requirement, but it does not necessarily demonstrate that the policy is implemented or effective.

Authority: Who Has the Right to Decide?

Authority is a fundamental component of governance that is sometimes overlooked in cybersecurity discussions.

Organizations frequently concentrate on access permissions: who can access a system, modify a configuration, retrieve information, or perform an administrative function.

But possessing the technical ability to perform an action does not necessarily establish the legitimate authority to perform it.

Consider a systems administrator who possesses sufficient privileges to delete an organization's production database. Those privileges may be necessary for maintenance, recovery, or other legitimate operational responsibilities.

However, possessing those privileges does not mean the administrator is authorized to delete the database whenever they choose.

The legitimacy of such an action depends on organizational policies, assigned responsibilities, approved objectives, applicable circumstances, and established decision-making procedures.

This distinction exists throughout an organization:

  • An administrator may implement a security control without having the authority to redefine organizational security policy.
  • A security analyst may identify and report a risk without having the authority to accept that risk on behalf of the organization.
  • A department manager may request access to information without having the authority to approve access to restricted data.
  • An automated system may possess the capability to execute a consequential action without having been authorized to do so under the current circumstances.

Cybersecurity frameworks should help organizations recognize and assess these distinctions.

Without clearly established authority, technical permissions can inadvertently become substitutes for organizational decision-making.

Delegation Does Not Eliminate Accountability

Organizations cannot operate effectively if every decision requires direct approval from executive leadership or a governing body.

Authority must therefore be delegated.

Delegation allows individuals, departments, service providers, and automated systems to perform defined functions within established boundaries.

Effective delegation should establish:

  • Who is delegating authority.
  • Who or what receives that authority.
  • The purpose for which authority is granted.
  • The actions and resources within its scope.
  • Applicable restrictions and conditions.
  • The circumstances under which authority expires or may be revoked.
  • The responsibilities for oversight and accountability.

Delegating authority does not eliminate the accountability of those responsible for governing the organization.

An organization may outsource its cybersecurity operations to a managed security service provider, for example, but that arrangement does not automatically transfer the organization's governance responsibilities, regulatory obligations, or accountability for risk decisions.

Similarly, delegating operational decisions to artificial intelligence does not eliminate the responsibilities of the humans who authorize, deploy, and oversee those systems.

Delegation changes who may exercise authority. It does not, by itself, change who remains accountable for organizational governance.

Oversight: Ensuring Governance Remains Effective

Policies and delegated authority establish the conditions under which an organization should operate. Oversight helps determine whether those conditions continue to be satisfied.

Organizations change continuously. Employees assume new responsibilities, technologies are introduced, suppliers change, threats evolve, and business objectives are revised.

Governance must account for those changes.

Effective oversight may include reviewing security performance, assessing control effectiveness, examining exceptions, monitoring risk exposure, reviewing privileged access, and evaluating whether delegated responsibilities remain appropriate.

Oversight should also provide mechanisms for identifying and addressing deviations from established expectations.

However, oversight is not simply the collection of information.

An organization may produce extensive security reports, dashboards, audit logs, and risk assessments without meaningfully acting upon their findings.

Effective oversight requires that relevant information reaches individuals with the authority and responsibility to evaluate it, make decisions, and direct appropriate corrective action.

Information without accountability provides visibility, but it does not necessarily provide effective governance.

When Governance Breaks Down

Governance failures do not always originate from the absence of policies or technical security controls.

They may arise from a disconnect between organizational expectations, technical capabilities, delegated authority, and operational oversight.

Consider an organization that has implemented multifactor authentication, privileged access management, endpoint detection and response, network segmentation, and comprehensive security monitoring.

These controls may provide substantial protection against unauthorized access and malicious activity.

However, important governance questions remain:

  • Who determines whether a consequential action is authorized?
  • Can an individual approve an action outside their delegated responsibilities?
  • Can an exception become a permanent operating practice without appropriate review?
  • Can an automated system expand its activities beyond its authorized purpose?
  • Who decides whether an identified risk is acceptable?
  • How does the organization demonstrate that these decisions were legitimate?

These questions are not necessarily resolved by implementing additional security technologies.

They require governance mechanisms supported by appropriate technical, procedural, and organizational controls.

As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly integrated into organizational operations, the distinction between capability and authority becomes even more significant.

A system capable of independently reasoning, selecting actions, and interacting with organizational resources introduces governance challenges that extend beyond traditional access management.

An authorized objective does not necessarily authorize every available method of achieving it.

Governance Within Cybersecurity Frameworks

Established cybersecurity frameworks address governance through different structures and approaches.

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0, for example, establishes Govern as one of its six core functions, addressing organizational context, risk management strategy, roles and responsibilities, policies, oversight, and cybersecurity supply chain risk management.

ISO/IEC 27001 incorporates governance-related responsibilities through leadership, organizational roles, information security objectives, risk management, and performance evaluation.

COBIT distinguishes governance from management, recognizing that directing and evaluating organizational objectives differs from planning, implementing, and operating the activities necessary to achieve them.

Although these frameworks approach governance differently, they reinforce a common organizational need: cybersecurity must be directed and overseen as an organizational responsibility rather than treated exclusively as a technical function.

The effectiveness of any framework depends, in part, on how its governance expectations are translated into organizational practices and supported through implementation and assessment.

The JAZB Perspective

Within the JAZB research initiative, governance is examined through two complementary framework families: the JAZB AI Governance & Enforcement Framework and the JAZB Cybersecurity Framework.

JAZB AI Governance & Enforcement Framework

The JAZB AI Governance & Enforcement Framework approaches governance as an architectural discipline, emphasizing the distinction between technical capability and legitimate Authority.

Within JAZB, established human governance determines the Law under which organizational actors and automated systems operate. Delegated Authority establishes the boundaries within which consequential actions may be performed.

The framework is designed around the principle that an actor's ability to perform an action does not independently establish its Authority to perform that action.

This distinction becomes especially important when considering autonomous AI systems.

An AI system may identify an alternative method of accomplishing an authorized objective. However, its ability to discover or execute that method does not independently authorize actions outside its established Scope, purpose, or governing Law.

JAZB examines how these boundaries can be represented architecturally, evaluated at relevant enforcement points, and supported through evidence and accountability mechanisms.

JAZB Cybersecurity Framework

The JAZB Cybersecurity Framework approaches governance through organizational requirements, assessable outcomes, and evidence-based assurance.

Its dedicated Governance Core establishes thirteen governance outcomes addressing responsibilities such as:

  • Organizational accountability and competence.
  • Risk appetite and tolerances.
  • Delegated Authority.
  • Third-party dependencies.
  • Evidence and assurance.
  • Oversight effectiveness.
  • Incident determinations and disclosure.
  • Crisis decision rights.
  • Continuous improvement.

These outcomes connect governing-body responsibilities to underlying cybersecurity requirements and their assessment results.

The objective is to establish a structured relationship between what an organization is expected to govern, how those expectations are implemented, and what evidence supports the resulting assurance determinations.

Neither the existence of policies nor the implementation of technical controls is independently sufficient to demonstrate effective organizational governance.

The framework remains a public research edition, with independent validation and controlled enterprise implementation representing important subsequent stages of its development.

Final Thoughts

Governance is not simply the establishment of policies. It is the continuing organizational discipline of establishing direction, assigning authority, overseeing its exercise, and maintaining accountability for decisions and outcomes.

Policies establish expectations.

Authority establishes who may act.

Oversight helps determine whether actions remain consistent with organizational expectations.

Accountability establishes responsibility for the resulting decisions and outcomes.

Together, these principles provide a foundation for effective cybersecurity and AI governance.

But even when these elements are established, one important question remains:

How does an organization know that its governance is actually working?

That question introduces the next stage of our discussion: governance assurance.
